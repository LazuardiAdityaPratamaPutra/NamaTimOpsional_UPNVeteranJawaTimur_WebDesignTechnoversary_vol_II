## At home and at school

- Do not burn rubbish, and keep dry waste away from your yard.
- Save the emergency number 112 and your local disaster agency (BPBD) contact.
- Prepare a family plan: a meeting point, an evacuation route, and masks.

Download and print the preparedness checklist from the Checklist tab on the Learn page.

## In villages and communities

Indonesia's Ministry of Environment stresses that prevention must be strengthened from the ground level, because putting fires out is not enough. The front line is the Fire-Aware Community (Masyarakat Peduli Api): residents who recognize hazards, monitor conditions, and report the first signs of fire as early as possible.

On peatland, village reservoirs, wells, and canal blocks slow the loss of water. A canal block works like plugging a bathtub so the water does not keep draining out. Residents are also advised to clear flammable material around homes, keep a water reserve, know their evacuation routes, and not approach a fire without training.

## Managing land

Prof. Muhammad Adib recommends site-based peat management involving government, communities, academics, and businesses: researchers provide rewetting technology, local governments strengthen and enforce the rules, and residents manage water and watch over the land.

Clearing land without burning only lasts with support and incentives (Tacconi and Muttaqin, 2021, cited by the Ministry of Environment), because burning is cheap and fast. One way out is paludiculture, which means farming without draining peat, for example with jelutung, sago, or purun. Rewetting, replanting, and supporting livelihoods are the three steps of the Peatland Restoration Agency's 3R strategy.

## The role of technology

Satellites detect hotspots, which are pixels with a temperature anomaly. According to NASA, VIIRS pixels are about 375 meters wide and MODIS pixels about 1 kilometer, and the center of a pixel is not necessarily the true location of the fire. BNPB monitors hotspots daily using satellite data from BMKG and the Ministry of Forestry.

But underground peat fires often slip past satellites, which makes reports from residents very valuable. Hotspot monitoring and simulation tools like PyraOps help us understand where a fire could spread. This technology complements, and never replaces, vigilance and real action on the ground.
