## Health impacts

Wildfire smoke carries fine particles (PM2.5) that are far smaller than the width of a human hair. According to WHO, these particles can penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream, and both short-term and long-term exposure are linked to heart and lung disease.

According to Indonesia's Ministry of Health, children are the most at-risk group because their lungs and cognitive systems are still developing. In older adults, extreme exposure can worsen chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and trigger heart and blood vessel problems. Pregnant women and people with existing conditions also need extra protection.

The scale is large. Koplitz and colleagues (2016) estimated that the 2015 haze caused about 100,300 premature deaths across Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. That figure comes from modeling, not a direct count. The World Bank reported that more than 900,000 people had respiratory illness during the 2019 fires.

## Ecosystem impacts

Burning peat releases huge amounts of carbon. In 2015, peat fires released carbon dioxide each day equal to the daily fossil fuel emissions of the entire European Union. Peat that burns away can also leave sinkholes in the ground.

Wildlife pays too. According to Prof. Raden Wisnu Nurcahyo of Gadjah Mada University, fire destroys the trees where orangutans feed and nest. Smoke and flames push them to the ground and toward farms and villages, raising the risk of starvation, burns, and conflict with people. Recorded deaths are probably lower than the real toll because animals deep in forests and peat are hard to detect.

## Social and economic impacts

The World Bank estimated that the 2019 fires cost Indonesia at least 5.2 billion US dollars, about 0.5 percent of gross domestic product. Of that, 157 million dollars was direct damage to assets, and the rest was lost economic activity.

Smoke does not stop at borders. Prof. Muhammad Adib notes that haze from Kalimantan can reach other countries, and the Koplitz study counted victims in three. The Ministry of Health reported that about 3 million people in 37 districts and cities were directly exposed to smoke in August 2026.

## Protecting yourself in the haze

- Cut back on time outdoors and keep outside air from coming in. Indoor air filters help.
- Wear an N95-type mask that filters fine particles, not a thin cloth mask.
- Check air quality in an official app before going out.
- Go to a clinic or hospital right away if you have shortness of breath, a cough that will not ease, chest pain, or severe eye irritation. The Halo Kemenkes hotline is 1500-567.

All of these impacts begin with fires that could have been prevented. The last article covers how.
