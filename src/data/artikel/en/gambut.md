## What peatland is

Peat is a pile of plant remains that never fully rotted because they stayed underwater for a very long time. Peat covers about 3 percent of the world's land but stores nearly a third of the world's soil carbon. Indonesia has the largest area of tropical peat in the world, about 13.43 million hectares: roughly 5.8 million in Sumatra, 4.5 million in Kalimantan, and 3 million in Papua.

## Wet is the safeguard, dry is the fuel

Wet peat is like a sponge full of water: hard to burn. Wring that sponge dry, and it becomes a pile of fuel. Dr. Elham Sumarga of ITB's School of Life Sciences and Technology explains that in natural conditions peat rarely burns because it stays wet. Once it loses its water, the dried organic matter becomes fuel in huge quantities.

One of the main reasons the water disappears is drainage canals. Canals dug for plantations and timber estates carry water out and lower the water table. The government uses a water table 0.4 meters below the surface as a threshold in cultivated areas (Government Regulation 57/2016). Some researchers argue the number works better as an early warning of hotspots than as a limit for damage, so the threshold is still debated.

## The fire you cannot see

Dry peat rarely burns with tall flames. It smolders slowly underground, like a mosquito coil. Prof. Guillermo Rein of Imperial College London and colleagues call these "zombie fires": they smolder unseen and can then re-emerge at the surface. They can last for weeks or months, and many only go out when the rainy season arrives.

They are hard to put out because the embers are inside the ground. Water poured on top can carve channels that bypass the embers, and finishing the job can take billions of liters. Michael Brady of CIFOR says water-bombing helicopters are ineffective because they simply cannot drop enough water. Satellites also often miss these fires, because underground embers do not always give off heat at the surface.

## The price: carbon

Page and colleagues (Nature, 2002) calculated that peat and forest fires in Indonesia in 1997 released 0.81 to 2.57 billion tonnes of carbon, equal to 13 to 40 percent of the world's yearly carbon emissions from fossil fuels. In their 2.5-million-hectare study area in Central Kalimantan, about 0.73 million hectares of peat burned. That carbon had been stored for centuries and was released within one fire season, like savings built up over decades spent in a single night.

## How to recover: rewet it

Indonesia's Peatland Restoration Agency uses a 3R strategy. Rewetting restores moisture through canal blocks, canal backfilling, and boreholes. Revegetation replants species that do not disturb the water cycle. Revitalization of livelihoods helps residents earn a living from the land without draining it. Dr. Elham Sumarga names jelutung, sago, and purun as examples of crops that grow without draining peat.

## Limits of the PyraOps model

The PyraOps simulation only describes surface fire. Spread below the peat surface is not modeled, so simulation results on peatland should be read as an educational picture, not an operational forecast.
