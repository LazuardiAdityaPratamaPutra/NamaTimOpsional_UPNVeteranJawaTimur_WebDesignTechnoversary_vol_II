## How a fire starts and spreads

A fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen, and heat. In forests and on open land the fuel is grass, shrubs, leaf litter, and peat. Oxygen is always in the air, so what matters is the source of heat and how dry the fuel is.

Once a fire has started, its speed depends on the fuel, the weather, and the shape of the terrain. Wind brings fresh oxygen and pushes the fire toward new fuel, and a slope makes a fire climb faster than it descends. The most important factor is how much water the fuel holds, because the fire's heat must first boil that water away. It is like lighting wet firewood: you burn through a lot of matches before it finally catches.

## The spark: almost always people

Almost all forest and land fires in Indonesia are started by people, either on purpose or through carelessness. Research by IPB forest-fire expert Prof. Bambang Hero Saharjo (1999) puts the figure at 99.9 percent. He also explains that burning has long been used to clear land, by smallholders and large companies alike.

The reason is economic. In a study by KKI Warsi and IPB's Faculty of Forestry in Jambi, clearing land by hand takes about eight months, while burning takes about one. The ash also adds phosphorus and lowers the acidity of peat soil, which saves on fertilizer. The cost that is saved does not disappear. It moves onto other people in the form of smoke.

Indonesian law prohibits clearing land by burning (Law 32/2009, Article 69 paragraph 1 letter h). There is an exception for local wisdom, limited to 2 hectares per family with local crop varieties and firebreaks, and it does not apply to companies.

## The role of the dry season and weather

The dry season does not light fires, but it makes fuel so dry that one spark is enough. BMKG, the national weather agency, expects El Niño to last into early 2027, with longer spells without rain, and calls September 2026 a critical phase in Sumatra and Kalimantan. The national disaster agency (BNPB) warns that fires that have been put out can flare up again when wind carries embers.

Prof. Muhammad Adib of Airlangga University puts it this way: El Niño is only a catalyst for drought, while the main axis is human-made and structural. In other words, the weather sets the stage, but people light the fire.

## What we can do

- Never burn land or rubbish.
- Fully put out campfires and embers before leaving.
- Report smoke or fire as early as possible to village officials, your local disaster agency (BPBD), or the emergency number 112.

The root of the problem is clear. On peatland, the same fire turns into a far harder problem to put out, and that is the topic of the next article.
